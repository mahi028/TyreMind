Why this is hard and not merely fiddly

The thing hiding the answer is the same size as the answer, and points the opposite way. Fuel is worth about as much as the tyre. Ignore it and you do not get a slightly wrong number — you get the wrong sign.

The brief, verbatim

"Create a predictive model that strips out external noise from practice sessions to generate clean tyre performance degradation curves. Includes post-race validation tools to compare predicted wear against actual race-day pace."

The honest core of the whole project

Public F1 data contains no measured tyre wear. Pirelli measures it, the FIA and teams see it, the public never does. We searched Pirelli media, FIA documents, Kaggle, Hugging Face, academic replication packages, OpenF1, Ergast and the timing schema itself. It does not exist.

So we cannot grade ourselves against truth on real data. Everything that follows is designed around that constraint instead of pretending it away.

The falsification argument, which is the part judges have not seen before

Most validation needs ground truth. This one does not. A method that reports tyres improving with age has refuted itself, and we can count how often that happens across four seasons of real racing. exp14 is a validation leg that survives the total absence of wear labels, and no paper in our literature review runs it.

And it is not a strawman — this is what mentors challenged us on

A mentor said "regression will never work". They were right, and this proves it. But we also built the fixed version: a pooled regression with fuel, track and traffic terms. It is rung 3 of our own ladder, it beats us at forecasting lap times, and we route that question to it. Slide 09.

We invented a traffic index, then validated it against measurement

Derived from lap start times alone, no positional data needed. Checked against OpenF1's measured 4 Hz gaps: median ρ = −0.798 across 66 races, correct sign in 66 of 66, worst single race −0.512, largest p-value 3×10⁻¹⁹. Stint-level agreement ρ = 0.912.

Synthetic — and why there are two generators

Generator 1 is deliberately hostile: Student-t noise where our estimator assumes Gaussian, and 25% scrubbed sets so tyre age does not start at zero.

Generator 2 exists because a generator shaped like the estimator cannot falsify it. Lap time comes from a line integral over a speed profile, fuel acts through mass, traffic through downforce loss. There is no rate parameter anywhere — the truth is a counterfactual, differenced.

The caveat we always state first: we wrote both. Generator 2 removes the functional-form advantage. It removes nothing else.

Seven cleaning filters, every one logged

Every session ships a receipt. Monza 2024: the API gave 1,008 laps, we kept 921, and the file says exactly where the other 87 went. Outliers by median + 3×MAD, not mean and standard deviation — a safety car inflates a standard deviation enough to hide itself.

The mathematics

Three things look identical in the data.
Naming them, and pinning each one, is the method.

y[d,t] = α[r] run intercept: car, setup, start fuel − A·g(t) track evolution, whole field + θ[t] residual track wobble + s[d,t] ← THE TYRE. What we want. Invisible. − φ·L[d,t] fuel burn-off, L = laps this run + γ·TI[d,t] traffic + ε noise Latent state, evolving in TYRE AGE not session lap: s[d,a+1] = s[d,a] + rate[d,a] + noise rate[d,a+1] = rate[d,a] + noise ← lets a cliff appear

We never tell the model what shape degradation has

Because rate is free to drift, a cliff needs no special machinery — it is rate accelerating. Everyone else forces a straight line. We measured 2,827 real stints: only 55.9% are linear. The field-standard model is the wrong shape for 44% of real stints.

① Fuel vs tyre — perfectly confounded

y = c + β(a₀+f) − φ·f = (c + β·a₀) + (β − φ)·f

The slope you can see is β − φ, not β. More data does not help; it is algebra. Pinned by a physical prior on φ whose own uncertainty widens every interval we publish.

② Track evolution vs a uniform rate shift

Pinned by making track evolution a saturating curve A(1−e−kL). Rubber deposition genuinely saturates; tyre wear does not. Physically correct and identifying.

③ Tyre age vs session lap — our best idea

Every car pits at a different time, so on lap 30 one car is on 3-lap-old tyres and another on 25-lap-old ones. The grid staggers itself. Watch one car and you cannot do this; watch twenty and it solves itself for free, with no assumption.

Cappello & Hoegh fit one driver in one race. They structurally cannot use this.

Where we lose, stated before anyone finds it

Lap-time forecasting: 4th of 9. A pooled regression wins at 0.4088 against our 0.6484, so we route that question to it. Real pit stops: a tie, 5.92 ± 0.52 laps against 5.98 ± 0.52 for the closest paper — intervals that overlap that far are the same result and we record it as a tie.

But the pooled regression that wins the lap-time table comes last on those same 274 pit stops at 11.12 laps, answering only half of them. Forecasting a lap time and explaining why it moved are different jobs.

It survives a truth we did not write

On the independent physics generator our margin is +0.0086 ± 0.0017, which is 2.6× larger than on our own generator. But we also report that "the degradation rate" is three different quantities on a non-linear generator, and a different model wins each.

The most damning number about the standard method

Naive regression claims 47.3% confidence on its pit window and delivers 2.5%. Ours claims 31.9% and delivers 24.0% — still overconfident by 7.9 points, and we say so. Best of six, not perfect.

How the probability is produced

Not a softmax dressed up as a probability. We simulate 1,200 races per candidate pit lap under common random numbers, so draw i read across all candidates is one coherent race, and we count the winners. That is a frequency.

How much is data, and how much is assumption

6.0% data-driven. Cramér–Rao bound 0.0161 against our 0.0221 — we sit at 1.38× the theoretical optimum. No paper in our review states this number. Cappello & Hoegh have the identical structure and never quantify their prior's contribution.

Two rules stop this being a way to launder a weak result

Every route cites a result file. The code refuses to construct a route without evidence — a route with no evidence is a preference, and preferences are how a system ends up routing to whatever its author likes.

A margin inside the noise is a tie, and ties break on honesty. Three models sit within one standard error on pit timing. Claiming a win on 0.06 laps would be exactly the cherry-picking this project exists to avoid.

The result we route away from, and why it matters more than the win

On predicting Sunday from Friday, every model on the bench scores negative skill — worse than ignoring practice and quoting that driver's own average. So the product declines the forecast rather than dressing a negative-skill number as a prediction.

We found that by building a test designed to kill our own earlier, flattering number. It worked.

"We tested nine models on five tasks. Three different models won. So we route each question to the one the evidence says is best — including away from our own model twice. Competitors ship one model and claim it wins everything."

The build fails if a route goes stale

Tests re-read the result files behind every route. Re-run an experiment, change a winner, and the build breaks rather than leaving the table asserting fiction.

Beyond motorsport · the NASA result

The same code, unmodified, runs on jet engines.

Strip the motorsport words away and this is not a tyre problem. It is: something wears out while it works, you cannot measure it directly, and several other things move the only signal you can see.

NASA C-MAPSS — the most-cited benchmark in predictive maintenance

Turbofan engines run to failure with published remaining-life labels, which is exactly what motorsport data does not have. We pointed the same estimator at it with no tyre-specific code.

Remaining-life error, RMSE22.66 cycles
Mean absolute error17.94 cycles
Engines scored100 of 100
Predicted early — the safe direction44%

Said plainly, because a judge will look it up

Published deep models reach 15.98. We are not state of the art on this benchmark and have never claimed to be. The claim is transfer: unmodified tyre code on jet engines, in the competitive range, with no retuning.

What is competitive is the uncertainty. A 2025 paper presents 93.5–95.2% coverage as a new contribution; our conformal layer reaches 95.2% over 69,206 laps.

What transferred with no change

  • The latent state model — a level and a rate that drift
  • Pooling a baseline across a fleet of units
  • The entire uncertainty machinery
  • Remaining-life projection to a threshold

What changed: "laps" became "flight cycles", "lap time" became a health index from 12 sensors, fuel and traffic switched off.

A physics check that costs nothing to show

We recover circuit rotation direction — clockwise or anticlockwise — from per-corner tyre energy alone, correct on 7 of 8 circuits. It uses no wear data. If the energy model were wrong, it would get this wrong.

Where this goes next

Aviation, wind turbines, rail braking, mining haul fleets. Any asset with a degrading component, no direct sensor, and a confounded observable.

Adaptive to any data — the precise claim

We need four fields: lap_time, compound, tyre_age, lap_number. Anything richer is a bonus, never a requirement.

Where it does not adapt, honestly: wet running is excluded and reported as excluded; kerb strikes are impact, not gradual wear, so we show them in the residual; traffic-heavy stints under-report by about a quarter.

Trustworthy enough to adopt

We built the more advanced model on purpose, to try to beat ourselves

A ten-state continuous-discrete Extended Kalman Filter: four wear states, four temperatures, thermal coupling, skewed-t noise, driven by measured per-corner loads. 1,967 lines, 118 tests, pre-registered before a line was written.

It converged on every one of 140 real stints. It still lost: 43% worse at carrying a compound parameter to a new circuit, at p = 2×10⁻⁶, and 304 s per session against a 60 s budget. So it does not ship.

And we caught our own circular test

The first run of that experiment said the new model was 28% better. It was scoring the model's own in-sample fit against an out-of-sample baseline. Fixing the leak moved the result by 71 points and flipped its sign.
